This is the first in an occasional series I'm calling "Lessons from the Trenches" —
real situations, lightly anonymized, from my years working with older adults and
the families who love them. My hope is that if you're navigating something
similar, you'll feel less alone in it, and maybe pick up an idea or two along
the way.

I want to start with something that still surprises families after all this
time: the assessment and matching process is part science, part art, and the
art half is the part nobody can fully explain in advance.

## The match that looked perfect on paper

A few years ago, we paired a retired English professor with a companion who had
a master's degree in literature, a gentle voice, and rave reviews from every
family she'd worked with. On paper, it was as close to a guaranteed fit as
we'd ever assembled. The first visit was polite. The second was shorter than
scheduled. By the third, the professor told his daughter he'd "rather just read
on his own, thanks."

Meanwhile, that same week, we sent a twenty-six-year-old former barista —
bubbly, no particular interest in books — to visit a retired physics professor
who had, by his daughter's account, "no patience for small talk." Ninety
minutes later they were still at the kitchen table, deep in an argument about
whether the Voyager probes would outlast the human race.

## What we've learned to look for

Credentials and shared interests matter, but they're not the whole story. Over
time, we've learned to pay closer attention to a handful of less obvious things
during an assessment:

- **Pace.** Some people want a conversation that moves. Others want long, easy
  pauses. Mismatched pace can feel like friction even when both people are
  perfectly pleasant.
- **Who needs to lead.** A few of our clients light up when someone else drives
  the conversation. Others want to be the one asking the questions — and feel
  managed if a companion arrives with too strong an agenda.
- **What "company" means to this particular person.** For some, it's
  conversation. For others, it's having someone nearby while they garden, or
  simply not eating lunch alone. We try to find out what they actually want
  company *for*, not just whether they want it.
- **The first five minutes.** I've started paying close attention to how
  someone responds in the doorway — not what they say, but whether their
  shoulders drop or square up. It tells you more than the intake form does.

## Why this matters for families

If a first visit doesn't go well, it's tempting to conclude that your parent
"isn't a good fit for this kind of thing." In our experience, that's rarely
the right read. More often, it just means the first match wasn't the right
match — and that's something we can keep working on, not a verdict on whether
companionship will work at all.

The professor who sent his literature-loving companion home? Six weeks later,
we tried again with someone who talked baseball more than books. They've been
meeting weekly for two years now. He still complains, mostly about the Giants'
bullpen — but he never misses a visit.

If you're in the middle of this process and the first try didn't click, that's
useful information, not a dead end. Tell us what felt off. The more honestly
families can describe *why* something didn't work, the faster we can find what
will.
