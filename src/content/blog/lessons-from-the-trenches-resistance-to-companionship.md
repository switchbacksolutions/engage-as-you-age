Almost every family that calls us has had some version of this conversation
with their parent:

> "I don't need a babysitter."
>
> "I'm not paying a stranger to sit in my living room."
>
> "I'm fine. I have the TV."

If that sounds familiar, take a breath — you are nowhere near the first family
to hit this wall, and it is rarely the dead end it feels like in the moment.

## It's not really about the companion

In almost twenty years of doing this work, I've come to believe that
resistance to companionship is hardly ever about the companion. It's about
what accepting one seems to mean.

For a lot of older adults, saying yes to a regular visitor feels like
admitting something they're not ready to admit — that they're slowing down,
that their world has gotten smaller, that their kids are starting to "manage"
them the way they once managed a teenager. Nobody wants to be the project.
Pride is doing a lot of the talking in that "I don't need anybody."

I think of one client — a former union organizer, sharp as a tack at
eighty-nine — who told his daughter flatly that he'd "negotiated harder
contracts than this and he wasn't signing." Three months later, he was
introducing his companion to his neighbors as "my friend Daniel, who happens
to also keep me company on Tuesdays." Same arrangement. Completely different
framing — and the framing is what let him say yes.

## What tends to help

A few things I've watched work, again and again:

- **Drop the word "care" if it's landing badly.** "Companionship,"
  "conversation partner," or even just "someone to do things with" can open a
  door that "caregiver" slams shut.
- **Start smaller than feels necessary.** A single one-hour visit, framed as a
  trial, is a much easier yes than "twice a week from now on." Most
  resistance softens once there's an actual person in the room rather than an
  abstract idea of one.
- **Let your parent be the one doing someone a favor.** "Could you help this
  person practice their Spanish / learn about gardening / find their way
  around the neighborhood?" reframes the visit from something being done *to*
  them to something they're contributing.
- **Don't argue the point — revisit it.** Pushing harder in the moment
  usually produces a more entrenched no. Coming back to it gently, a few weeks
  later, after the initial sting has faded, often gets a different answer.

## When it still doesn't move

Sometimes none of this works on the first attempt, or the third. That's worth
naming honestly: occasionally a parent simply isn't ready, and continuing to
push can do more harm to the relationship than good. In those cases, we've
found it helps to let the *idea* sit for a while rather than the *request* —
mentioning it occasionally and lightly, without making it the center of every
visit home.

If you're in the thick of this, I'd encourage you not to read a "no" as final.
In our experience, it's much more often a "not yet, and not framed that way."
Finding the right way to ask the question again is usually more productive
than asking it more forcefully.
