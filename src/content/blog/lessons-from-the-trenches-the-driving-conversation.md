Of all the conversations families bring to us for advice, the one about
driving may be the most fraught. More than moving out of a longtime home, more
even than hiring help, giving up the car keys tends to land as the moment
someone stops being seen as fully independent — by others, and often by
themselves.

## Why this one cuts so deep

For most of the adults I work with, driving isn't just a task to be checked
off. It's the spontaneous trip to see a grandchild, the Sunday drive with a
spouse, the simple ability to decide *right now* to go somewhere. Taking that
away — or being told it should be taken away — can feel like the first real
marker of old age, even to someone who has quietly adjusted to a dozen smaller
losses already.

I think of a client, a retired surgeon, who told me that losing his license
felt worse than retiring from the operating room. "At least I chose that," he
said. "Nobody else got to decide when I stopped operating."

That's the heart of it: control. The conversation goes badly when it feels
like something being decided *about* someone. It goes better — not easily,
but better — when the person feels like a participant in deciding it.

## What we've seen work

- **Start before it's an emergency.** The hardest version of this conversation
  happens in a hospital parking lot after an accident. The easier version
  happens months earlier, over coffee, as one option among several — "let's
  think through what happens if driving gets harder down the road," not "you
  need to stop now."
- **Bring in a third party.** A doctor, an occupational therapist who does
  driving evaluations, or even a longtime family friend can sometimes deliver
  the message in a way that lands as guidance rather than control. It's much
  easier to hear "the doctor recommended it" than "your kids decided."
- **Solve the *next* problem, not just this one.** "You shouldn't drive
  anymore" creates an immediate, very real fear: *how do I get to my
  appointments, my friends, the grocery store?* Walking in with answers —
  a list of rideshare options, a regular companion who can drive to outings,
  a standing arrangement with a neighbor — turns a loss into a transition.
- **Acknowledge the grief.** This is a real loss, and it deserves to be
  treated like one. Letting someone be sad or angry about it, without rushing
  to reassure them it's "for the best," often does more to ease the
  transition than any amount of problem-solving.

## A small reframe that sometimes helps

One family I worked with stopped saying "Dad can't drive anymore" and started
saying "Dad has a driver now." It sounds like a small thing. It wasn't. Their
father began describing his companion — who picked him up twice a week for
errands and outings — as "my guy," with a note of pride rather than
resignation. The facts hadn't changed. The story around them had.

If you're staring down this conversation, know that there's rarely a clean,
single moment where it goes perfectly. It's usually a series of smaller
conversations, revisited with patience, that get you there. And it gets easier
when the person on the other side of the table has a real say — even if the
ultimate answer is the same one you walked in with.
