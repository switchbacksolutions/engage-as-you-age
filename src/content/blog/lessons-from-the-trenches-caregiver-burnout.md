I want to start this one with something I hear constantly, in almost the exact
same words, from sons and daughters all over the Bay Area:

> "I shouldn't be this tired. I shouldn't need a break. This is my mother —
> shouldn't I want to do this?"

If you've thought some version of that thought, I'd like to gently push back
on it. Wanting relief doesn't mean you love someone less. It means you're
human, and you've been carrying something heavy for a long time.

## What burnout actually looks like

It rarely arrives as a single dramatic moment. It shows up as the small things
that pile up quietly:

- Snapping at a parent over something trivial, then feeling crushed with guilt
  about it five minutes later
- Skipping your own doctor's appointments because there's "no time"
- Realizing you can't remember the last time you did something purely for
  yourself — not for your parent, not for your kids, just for you
- A low hum of resentment that scares you, because it doesn't match how you
  feel about the person you're caring for

None of that means you're failing. It means the role you're in is genuinely
demanding — often more demanding than a full-time job, with none of the
boundaries a job comes with.

## The myth that makes it worse

The belief that does the most damage, in my experience, is this one: *if I
really loved them, I wouldn't need help.* It's almost the opposite of true.
The caregivers who burn out hardest tend to be the ones who care the most —
because they keep giving long after their own reserves are empty, and they
interpret the exhaustion as a personal failing rather than a natural
consequence of an unsustainable pace.

I worked with a woman — a former ICU nurse, no less, someone who had spent her
whole career caring for people under pressure — who broke down in our office
admitting she'd started dreading her visits to her own father. She thought
that meant something was wrong with her. What it actually meant was that she'd
been doing the job of three people, alone, for two years, without a single
day off.

## What tends to help, in practice

- **Name a specific need, not a vague one.** "I need help" is hard to act on.
  "I need someone with Dad on Tuesday and Thursday afternoons so I can keep my
  job" is something we — or anyone — can actually solve.
- **Build in regular relief, not just emergency relief.** A standing weekly
  visit from a companion does more for a caregiver's well-being over time than
  an occasional emergency fill-in, because it gives you something to count on
  — a guaranteed pocket of time that's actually yours.
- **Let someone else do it differently than you would.** One of the hardest
  parts of stepping back is watching someone else's approach not match your
  own. It often isn't wrong — just different. Loosening that grip, even a
  little, is often what makes consistent help possible at all.
- **Talk to someone who has been where you are.** Caregiver support groups —
  in person or online — exist because this experience is far more common, and
  far less isolating, than it feels at two in the morning.

## The reframe I'd offer

Bringing in support isn't a sign that you've stopped being the person who
cares most. It's often what allows you to keep being that person — for
longer, and with more of yourself intact. The goal was never to do this alone.
It was to make sure your parent is well cared for, and that includes you.
